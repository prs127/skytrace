How Skytrace works

The data. SPHEREx maps the whole sky every six months in 102 near-infrared bands (challenge statement). Skytrace finds every SPHEREx spectral image covering your position with IRSA's SIA2 service, searching both spherex_dr1 and spherex_qr3 as the SPHEREx archive guide recommends. It reads only the needed pixels of each file from IRSA's AWS copy, using the file's cloud_access entry. It then subtracts the mission's zodiacal-light model (ZODI extension) and removes pixels flagged bad in the FLAGS extension. Finally it resamples every exposure onto one north-up grid with SPHEREx's 6.15″ pixel scale.

Why "visits". Each SPHEREx detector sees a different wavelength across its surface (WCS-WAVE extension), so every exposure sees a star at a different colour; see the IRSA introduction notebook. A raw time-ordered movie therefore flickers because of colour, not motion. Skytrace stacks exposures by visit (clusters in time), stacks each detector separately and averages the detectors with equal weight, and offers same-channel comparisons.

The 102 channels. Channel numbers, wavelength ranges and resolving powers come from the official SPHEREx DR1 SPECTRAL_CHANNELS table of the DR1 all-sky cube used in the IRSA HiPS notebook. Each exposure's channel is read from SPHEREx's official per-pixel channel map (spectral_channels calibration product) at the target's detector pixel; the exact wavelength there comes from the WCS-WAVE extension. Pixels the map leaves outside all 17 channels of a detector are shown in the spectrum at their measured wavelength but are not given a channel number.

Motion and distance. The target's predicted position in every exposure includes its proper motion and its annual parallax (the shift caused by Earth orbiting the Sun), computed from Earth's position on that date. Gaia stars in the overlay are moved the same way. Parallaxes come from Gaia DR3; for WISE 0855, which Gaia cannot see, Skytrace measured it from 277 NEOWISE detections: 438 ± 36 mas (a distance of 2.3 pc), consistent with the published 438.9 ± 3.0 mas.

Spectrum and light curve. The target is measured in every exposure on the original detector pixels, with the same method as IRSA's SPHEREx Source Discovery Tool tutorial: each pixel is converted from MJy/sr to µJy with SPHEREx's official solid-angle pixel map, then a 3-pixel-radius aperture (photutils, sub-pixel sampling) minus the sigma-clipped median of a 7–16 pixel annulus; the error comes from the VARIANCE extension. Our one added step: dividing by the fraction of light inside the aperture, measured from SPHEREx's own ePSFs (98.6–99.6%). Our code reproduces the tutorial's functions to better than one part in 100,000 on the same pixels.

Overlays and other missions. Gaia DR3 (ESA), CatWISE2020 (NASA WISE/NEOWISE) and 2MASS (NASA/IPAC) catalogues, all via IRSA. The spectrum also shows the target's 2MASS J/H/Ks and WISE W1/W2 brightness, matched at each catalogue's own date using the target's motion; for ordinary stars they agree with SPHEREx to within a few percent. Gaia stars and the target are moved to each frame's date using their catalogue proper motions. WISE 0855 is not in Gaia; its motion was fitted to NEOWISE single-exposure detections (NASA). Flags show SPHEREx's own saturation (OVERFLOW, NONLINEAR), artefact (GHOST, HALO, BLOOM, STREAK, CROSSTALK) and masked pixels.

From WiseView. Skytrace borrows WiseView's ideas (source): blinking time windows, difference images, the epoch legend (+ added, − subtracted, · unused) and shift-and-add along a chosen proper motion.

Sky map, Aladin and reports. The "Where in the sky" map is a Mollweide projection of RA/Dec with the ecliptic and the Milky Way plane computed from standard J2000 constants; the little rocket only shows where you are going. The Aladin tab uses Aladin Lite (CDS) on IRSA's SPHEREx DR1 all-sky HiPS cube, which combines all epochs, so motion is seen in the main viewer, not there. "Report a candidate" writes up the current view from data already on screen.

Limits. Barnard's Star and R Draconis saturate SPHEREx, so their photometry is withheld (their images and motion are still shown). Motions are small compared with the 6.15″ pixels: Barnard's Star moves about 1.7 pixels per year, and parallax shifts are at most 0.09 pixel. Stacked frames average many channels, so they show positions, not spectra. In single-channel views the wavelength is exact at the target and drifts by up to half a channel at the corners of the full 9.1′ view. Displayed images are resampled bilinearly (measurements are not: they use the original pixels). The mover finder is experimental. For science-grade fluxes use IRSA's SPHEREx Spectrophotometry Tool.
