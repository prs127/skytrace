How to use Skytrace

Skytrace shows NASA SPHEREx infrared images of one patch of sky as a flip-book, so you can see what moves or changes between visits, then measure it. This page explains every control in the dashboard, including the ones that are easy to miss. In the dashboard, every panel also has a ? button with a short version of this page.

1. The screen at a glance

Top bar
Page links (Home, Dashboard, How to use), the search box with its field size menu, a badge saying whether the live IRSA archive is connected, Share and Export PDF.
Library (left)
The all-sky map, the example targets, your saved places in named folders, and your searches. « hides it; » Library brings it back.
Image (centre)
On your first visit a slim New here? line above the image lists the first four steps (pick a target, play, turn on Gaia DR3, open Moving objects); ✕ hides it for good. Then: the field name (the i button next to it explains the field; a yellow ! appears when a catalogued star is close enough to blend with the target), the Layers chips, the image with its play controls and visit buttons, and beside the image the frame details, the What each frame shows panel, the position read-out and ☆ Save this place. The 102-channel strip is underneath.
Analysis (right)
Tabs for the spectrum, light curve, moving objects, exposures, atlas, visit settings, sharing and data sources. » hides it and the image grows into the space; the « Analysis button at the top right of the image brings it back.

2. Your first five minutes

  1. In the Library, open Challenge targets and click WISE 0855-0714.
  2. Press ▶ Play. Three frames, one per SPHEREx visit, play in a loop. The yellow crosshair marks where WISE 0855 should be on each date; watch the faint dot inside it shift slightly to the right (west): about 1.2 pixels between the first and last visit.
  3. Click the Gaia DR3 chip above the image. Stars from the Gaia DR3 catalogue (the brightest 400 in the field, down to G = 20) get a blue circle. WISE 0855 has none: it is too cold and faint for Gaia.
  4. Open the Moving objects tab on the right. The search reports one object, labelled as the target, moving about 7″ a year.
  5. Switch on Difference in the panel beside the image. Stars that did not move disappear; a moving object leaves a bright and a dark spot side by side.
  6. Open the Spectrum tab: each dot is WISE 0855's brightness in one SPHEREx image, from 0.75 to 5 µm.

3. Search box and field size

Type a name or coordinates and press Search or Enter.

  • Names: the challenge targets and their common short forms (e.g. WISE 0855, Barnard, Epsilon Indi B, RW Aur, R Dra, M101).
  • Coordinates: RA then Dec, in degrees (133.795 -7.245) or in hours and degrees (08h55m10.8s -07d14m42s or 08:55:10.8 -07:14:42). Out-of-range or unreadable input is refused with a message under the box.
  • If the position falls inside a field already on the server, Skytrace opens that field and pins the position instead of downloading again.
  • Otherwise the server searches NASA's IRSA archive for the SPHEREx images covering that spot (up to 700 per search, spread evenly in time), in all current data releases, and downloads only the needed pixels. A message shows its progress. A 9′ field with 300–400 images usually takes a few minutes; how long depends on the field and on how quickly NASA IRSA answers.
  • Field size (9′, 15′, 20′) sets how much sky a new search downloads. A bigger field can follow faster-moving objects for longer, but it takes longer to download (about 1.6 times for 15′ and 2.2 times for 20′). Pre-built examples are 9.1′.
On the copy of Skytrace without a live server (GitHub Pages), only the pre-built examples open; new searches send you to the live site.

4. Library

The Library works like bookmark folders. Click a folder name to open or close it, and click a place to load it.

  • Challenge targets: the five objects named in the challenge (WISE 0855-0714, Barnard's Star, Epsilon Indi B, RW Aurigae, R Draconis) and a control field, M101. They are pre-built and open in about a second.
  • Fastest-moving stars: the 25 stars with the largest proper motion in ESA's Gaia DR3 catalogue (over 3″ a year), with distance and brightness. Clicking one that is not built yet starts a live search that also uses its Gaia motion and parallax, so the crosshair follows it.
  • Saved places: your bookmarks, in folders you name (a folder called "My places" is there to start). Click ☆ Save this place beside the image to save the whole field, or first click the image to pin a spot and save just that spot (📍). Choose a folder or make a new one, and add a note. In the Library, hover a saved place to ⇄ move it to another folder, ✎ rename it or ✕ remove it; hover a folder to ⤴ copy a link that shares the whole folder, rename it or delete it. Whoever opens a folder link gets a copy of the folder in their own Saved places. + New folder makes an empty folder.
  • Your searches: every new place you search or click on the sky map is added here (the challenge targets stay in their own folder). ✎ renames it and ✕ removes it from your list.

Saved places, names and your searches are stored only in your browser: nothing is uploaded, and clearing the browser's site data removes them. Use folder links or PDF exports to keep or share them.

5. Where in the sky

The oval is the whole sky (RA across, Dec up). The ring marks the field on screen. The dashed line is the ecliptic, the path of the Sun and planets, where most asteroids and comets are. The light band is the plane of the Milky Way, where stars are crowded. Blue diamonds are the challenge targets; grey ones are other places searched on this server. Click anywhere to search that spot (a small rocket flies there while it loads). A click is not aimed at any particular object, so Skytrace centres the new field on the nearest star in Gaia DR3 (brighter than G = 18, within 90″ of the click) and measures that star; the field is named after it and its i note says where you clicked and how far away the star is. If no such star is that close, it measures the exact spot you clicked. Typed coordinates are always measured exactly where you typed them.

6. Reading the image

  • North is up and east is left, as on a sky chart. One SPHEREx pixel is 6.15″; the full pre-built view is 9.1′ across.
  • The caption beside the image, above the What each frame shows panel, names the frame (for example "Visit 2 (2/3)"), its dates, how many SPHEREx images were stacked, and their wavelength range.
  • Move the mouse over the image to read RA and Dec (degrees and hours/degrees), the brightness in MJy/sr above the local sky, any SPHEREx flags at that pixel, and the nearest catalogued star.
  • Click to pin a position (red ×). The pin box lets you copy it in degrees or hours/degrees, put it into the search box, or clear it. The pin is saved in links and reports.

7. Playing the frames

  • ◀ and ▶ step one frame; ▶ Play loops through all frames. The long slider jumps to any frame.
  • Speed: drag the star to the right for faster playback, to the left for slower.
  • The visit buttons under the slider jump to a visit. The line "Visits used" shows which visits are in the frame on screen: + added, − subtracted, · not used.

8. What each frame shows

The buttons in the What each frame shows panel beside the image decide which SPHEREx images make up each frame. About this mode under them explains the current choice.

Visits (default)
SPHEREx comes back to each spot about every six months. Each frame stacks all images from one visit. Best for seeing motion between visits.
Same band
Each frame uses only the images taken in one SPHEREx channel (one colour). Each SPHEREx detector sees a different colour at every point, so comparing the same channel stops colour changes from looking like motion. A slider picks the channel (1–102) and a tolerance (± channels) to include neighbours. Clicking the channel strip also switches to this mode.
λ scan
Plays one visit through its colours from short to long wavelength: a spectrum movie that shows which sources are bright where. Choose the visit in the menu that appears.
Exposures
Every single image in time order. Stars flicker because each image is a different colour; fast solar-system objects can jump between images taken hours apart.
Add / subtract
See section 11.

9. Detectors

SPHEREx has six detectors (D1–D6) covering 0.75–5.0 µm, each split into 17 channels. The detector menu chooses which to stack: all six (the default), the short ones (D1+D2+D3), the long ones (D5+D6), a single detector, or false colour, where blue is D1+D2, green D3+D4 and red D5+D6. Very cold brown dwarfs such as WISE 0855 are brightest in D5+D6. When several detectors are stacked, each detector is stacked first and then they are averaged with equal weight, so every visit has the same colour mix.

10. Difference images

Switch on Difference to subtract a reference from every frame, so only changes remain. Choose the reference in the menu next to it: minus first frame, minus previous frame, or minus mean of other frames. A moving object leaves a bright spot where it is now and a dark spot where it was; a star that changed brightness leaves a single bright or dark spot. The blue-red colour map in the Display menu shows positive and negative clearly. Keyboard: D.

11. Adding and subtracting visits

Choose Add / subtract. A list of visits appears: tick visits in column A to add them together and in column B to subtract them. The frame shows the stack of all A images minus the stack of all B images. Leave B empty to just add visits for a deeper image. Adding visits together lowers the noise; subtracting an early visit from a late one shows what moved or changed.

12. Field of view

Two settings, for two different things:

  • View size (menu in the panel beside the image) zooms into the field already downloaded: the whole field, or 9.1′ (for bigger fields), 6′, 4′ or 2.5′. Instant.
  • Field size (next to the search box) decides how much sky a new search downloads: 9′, 15′ or 20′. Faster-moving objects stay inside a bigger field for longer; the cost is a longer download.

13. Display menu

These options change only how the picture is drawn, never the data or the measurements.

  • Stretch: how brightness maps to grey. asinh (default) shows faint and bright stars together; linear is literal; sqrt and log bring up faint detail.
  • Colour map: grey, inferno, viridis, or blue-red for difference images (blue negative, red positive).
  • Black point % and White point %: the brightness percentiles shown as black and white. Raise the black point to hide noise; lower the white point to see faint things.
  • Invert: dark stars on a light sky, as on old photographic plates.
  • Smooth pixels: blends neighbouring pixels on screen. Off by default, so you see SPHEREx's real 6.15″ pixels.
  • Flatten background (on by default): removes smooth glow (zodiacal light, scattered light) by subtracting the median of 15×15-pixel blocks. Stars are too small to be affected.
  • Same brightness scale for every frame (on by default): all frames use one scale, so a star that brightens really looks brighter. Turn it off to stretch each frame on its own.

14. Layers

The chips above the image switch markers on (✓) and off. Hover a chip for a reminder. The less obvious details are:

Target (yellow crosshair, on by default)
Where the target should be on that frame's date, computed from its catalogue position, proper motion and annual parallax (the small yearly wobble caused by Earth's orbit). If the object is where the crosshair says, the catalogue motion is right.
Gaia DR3 (blue circles)
Stars in ESA's Gaia DR3 catalogue, each moved to the frame's date with its own proper motion and parallax. The overlay holds the brightest 400 Gaia stars in the field (G < 20), so a dot with no circle is not necessarily missing from Gaia: it may be fainter than that, too red for Gaia, or new. Check before claiming anything.
CatWISE (orange squares)
Infrared sources from NASA's CatWISE2020 catalogue (WISE and NEOWISE), placed with their measured motion. Cold brown dwarfs that Gaia misses are often here.
2MASS (white diamonds)
Sources from the 2MASS survey placed where they were in 1997–2001 and not moved. A fast-moving star has left its diamond behind: the gap shows its motion over 25 years.
Flags
SPHEREx's own warnings for each pixel, shaded on the image: red saturated (too bright to measure), yellow artefact (ghost, halo, streak, crosstalk, persistence and similar), grey masked (no usable data). A pixel is shaded when at least half of the stacked images flag it. Do not trust anything that appears only inside a shaded area.
Movers (pink)
Tracks found by the moving-object search (section 19): a dot for each visit and an arrow from the first to the last position.
Compass (on by default)
North (N) and east (E) arrows and a bar 1′ long.

15. Compare with older surveys

The Compare with buttons at the top of the image show the same place, at the same centre and size, in older sky surveys: DSS2 optical photographs (1950s–1990s), 2MASS near-infrared (1997–2001) and AllWISE mid-infrared (2010–2011). A fast-moving star sits somewhere else in each; a cold brown dwarf is invisible in DSS2 but bright in AllWISE. The pictures are NASA IRSA's copies of these surveys, drawn with Aladin Lite: scroll or use − / + to zoom. A short note about the survey appears beside the picture (never on it). While you compare, the frame controls, play buttons and layers are paused, because they belong to the SPHEREx frames. Click SPHEREx or Back to the SPHEREx frames to return.

16. The 102-channel strip

Under the image, each column is one SPHEREx channel from 0.75 µm (left) to 5.0 µm (right), grouped by detector D1–D6. A filled column means this field has images in that channel. Hover to see the channel's wavelength range, resolving power and how many images it has; click a column to blink that channel across visits (Same band mode).

17. Spectrum

The target's brightness (µJy) at every wavelength SPHEREx measured, built on the fly from every image. Each dot is one image, coloured by visit; the ring marks images in the frame on screen.

  • Measured on the original detector pixels at the target's predicted position, with the same method as NASA IRSA's SPHEREx Source Discovery Tool tutorial (one stricter step: every pixel that adds to the aperture sum is checked for flags, not only those whose centre is inside it, because a cosmic ray just outside the notebook's check once added 225 times the star's light): a 3-pixel-radius aperture minus the local sky, corrected for the light outside the aperture using SPHEREx's own point-spread function.
  • Outliers: a value that SPHEREx did not flag but that is far from the same visit's other measurements at nearly the same wavelength (more than 5 times the local scatter and 3 times its own error; within 4 % in wavelength and 15 days) is marked outlier and treated like a flagged one. These are usually cosmic-ray hits the pipeline missed. In tests on 16 fields this marked 6 of about 3,000 values.
  • Hide points with flagged pixels (on by default): as in the tutorial, a measurement whose aperture contains any flagged pixel is left out. Switch it off to show them as hollow points.
  • Mark molecular bands: dotted lines at the wavelengths of common water (1.4, 1.9, 2.7 µm; ice 3.05 µm), methane (2.3, 3.3 µm), carbon dioxide (4.27 µm) and carbon monoxide (2.3, 4.67 µm) bands, the molecules the challenge mentions. They show where to look; a line is not a detection.
  • Warnings above the numbers: the tab says when catalogued stars are close enough for their light to fall in the 18.5″ measuring circle, and when SPHEREx's values differ by more than 2 times from the 2MASS or WISE catalogue brightness of the star at the target. Then the plot is the light inside the circle, not one star's spectrum.
  • Log flux axis: useful for very red objects such as brown dwarfs, whose brightness spans a large range.
  • White diamonds are 2MASS J, H, Ks and orange squares are WISE W1, W2 (from CatWISE2020), converted to µJy and matched to the target at each catalogue's own date.
  • For stars that saturate SPHEREx (Barnard's Star, R Draconis) the measurements are withheld, as the tutorial's rule requires.

18. Light curve

Brightness against date with the colour taken out: each clean measurement is divided by the median of the target's measurements in the same channel. A steady star stays on the dotted line at 1; a variable star moves above and below it. It needs at least two clean measurements in a channel, and the target must be clearly detected there (median brightness at least 5 times its error): at an empty position the measurements are sky noise, and dividing noise by its own median would draw large changes that mean nothing, so the tab says no source is detected instead. Stars too bright for SPHEREx get no light curve: Barnard's Star and R Draconis saturate in every image and RW Aurigae in most (only 8 usable points), and a saturated value is not a measurement of brightness, so plotting it would show changes that are not real. The tab says when this is the case. Their images still blink normally.

19. Moving objects

An automatic search for objects that move between visits, with strict tests so that noise is not reported as motion.

  1. Each visit is stacked, and sources are found in each stack separately.
  2. How precisely positions can be measured is worked out on the field itself, from Gaia DR3 stars that cannot move measurably. The visits are also lined up on those stars.
  3. The search uses the detectors chosen in the menu beside the image, so it changes when you change them. WISE 0855 opens in D5+D6, where it is bright; with all six detectors averaged it is too faint to pass the tests.
  4. Sources are linked across visits along straight lines. An object is reported only if it is seen in at least three visits (two points always fit a line), the straight line fits, the motion is at least 5σ and at least half a pixel in total, it is seen in at least two thirds of the visits that cover its path, its brightness changes by less than three times, and no other source sits within 2 pixels.

Each result gives the motion in arcseconds per year with its uncertainty, the significance, and how many visits it was seen in. It is labelled the target, known star Gaia DR3 … (with the catalogue motion to compare), or no match in the Gaia DR3 overlay: check it by eye (the overlay holds the brightest 400 Gaia stars in the field, so no match does not prove an object is unknown). Click a result to pin it; switch on the Movers layer to see its track. Under the list, a line says how many sources were tested and why the others were not reported.

To check a new candidate: blink it with Difference on, look for it in Compare with AllWISE and 2MASS, and use Shift-and-add with its motion. A real mover becomes a sharp dot.
Saturated stars and their surroundings are skipped. Fast solar-system objects that cross the whole field within one visit are not searched. With only two visits, motion cannot be tested.

20. Shift-and-add

In the Moving objects tab. Enter a motion in milliarcseconds per year (µα* east, µδ north; 1000 mas = 1″), or click use the target's motion, then switch it on. Every image is slid back along that motion before stacking, so an object moving that way stays still and builds up into a sharp dot while everything else smears. This is how faint movers too weak to see in one visit can be confirmed.

21. Exposures and flags

The table lists the SPHEREx images in the frame on screen: date (UTC), detector, wavelength at the target, channel, and SPHEREx's quality flags at the target's position (for example SOURCE, NONLINEAR, TRANSIENT). The FITS link in each row downloads the original SPHEREx file from IRSA (about 70 MB). The tab title shows how many images are in the frame; the table lists the first 150.

22. Atlas

An interactive map of the same place that you can zoom and pan, with the target and catalogue markers. The first menu picks the background: the SPHEREx all-sky map or AllWISE, 2MASS or DSS2 colour. For SPHEREx, the second menu picks any of its 100 channel maps (2 of the 102 channels are blank by design). The all-sky map combines all visits, so it cannot show motion: use the main image for that.

23. Visit grouping

The Visits tab sets how images are grouped into visits. A new visit starts after a gap longer than the first slider (default 30 days). A visit longer than the second slider (default 60 days) is split into equal parts; this matters near the ecliptic poles, which SPHEREx watches almost continuously (R Draconis has 9 visits this way).

24. Share, export and report

  • Copy link: a link that reopens exactly this view, including the frame, layers and settings.
  • Copy embed code: an <iframe> showing just the image and controls, for another website.
  • Copy centre RA/Dec: the field centre in degrees and in hours/degrees.
  • Save image (PNG): the frame on screen with its layers, at least 900 pixels wide, with a caption (target, frame, centre, field size, data credit).
  • Download data (CSV): every SPHEREx measurement of the target (date, detector, wavelength, channel, flux and error in µJy, flags, release, file link) plus the 2MASS and WISE points.
  • Report a candidate: writes up the view (link, pinned position, nearest catalogued stars, the images in the frame, moving-object results) with a checklist and room for your notes, to copy or download as text.

24b. Export PDF

Export PDF in the top bar opens a side panel for the place on screen (one place per PDF, so each report stays readable). Tick what to include, then press Download PDF:

  • Overview: target and field (position, motion, parallax, field size, number of images and visits, data releases), and the settings used with a link back to this exact view.
  • Images: the frame on screen, one image per visit (in Visits mode), a difference image (last visit minus first), the layers that are switched on, and the 102-channel coverage strip.
  • Measurements: the spectrum plot and, if you want, a table of every measurement; the light curve; the moving objects found with how they were tested; the visits table; the exposure list with flags; and a summary of SPHEREx quality flags at the target.
  • Your notes and checks: the pinned spot with the nearest catalogued stars, and any notes you type.
  • Data sources and credits.

Select all and Clear set every box at once. The PDF is made in your browser and saved as skytrace-<place>-report.pdf.

25. Sources and new data

The Sources tab lists where everything came from: the SPHEREx releases searched, how many images were found and used, the measuring method, the catalogues and the calibration files. On the live site, Check IRSA for new data releases compares the releases this field was built from with what IRSA has now and offers to rebuild it.

26. Links and embedding (URL)

Everything is in the link, so you can build one by hand. Examples:

  • app.html?ra=133.7869&dec=-7.2451 opens (or searches) that position.
  • app.html?ra=…&dec=…&pmra=…&pmdec=…&plx=…&epoch=57388&size=15 also sets the target's motion (mas/yr), parallax (mas), the catalogue date (MJD) and a 15′ field.
  • app.html?field=wise0855&det=lw&diff=1&ov=target,gaia opens a pre-built field in the long-wavelength detectors with difference images and two layers.
  • Add &embed=1 to show only the image and its controls.

Other settings: mode (epoch, band, scan, exp, custom), band, tol, win, span, stretch, cmap, lo, hi, fov, inv, smooth, lock, flat, sa, sapmra, sapmdec, dref, speed (milliseconds per frame), scan, A, B, frame, and pra/pdec for a pinned position.

27. Keyboard

  • Space: play or pause. ← →: previous or next frame. D: difference on or off.
  • Enter in the search box starts the search.

28. Limits

  • The moving-object search needs a total shift of at least half a pixel (3″) across the visits: about 3″ a year for visits one year apart, plus its other tests.
  • Motions are small compared with SPHEREx's 6.15″ pixels: Barnard's Star moves about 1.7 pixels a year, WISE 0855 about 1.3. They are seen best between visits a year apart.
  • Barnard's Star and R Draconis saturate SPHEREx, so their brightness and automatic motion measurements are withheld; their images and motion are still shown.
  • A stacked frame averages many channels: good for positions, not for spectra (use the Spectrum tab).
  • The image is resampled for display; every measurement uses the original pixels.
  • For research-grade brightness, use IRSA's own SPHEREx tools.

29. Glossary

RA / Dec
Sky coordinates: Right Ascension (like longitude, in hours or degrees) and Declination (like latitude, in degrees).
Arcminute (′), arcsecond (″)
1′ is 1/60 of a degree; 1″ is 1/60 of an arcminute. The full Moon is about 30′ across.
Visit
The images taken during one pass of SPHEREx over a spot, about every six months.
Detector, channel
SPHEREx has six detectors (D1–D6) over 0.75–5.0 µm, each with 17 channels: 102 in total.
µm
Micrometre: the wavelength of infrared light. Visible light is 0.4–0.7 µm.
Proper motion
How fast a star moves across the sky, in arcseconds or milliarcseconds (mas) per year. Nearby stars move most.
Parallax
The small yearly wobble of a nearby star caused by Earth's orbit. Its size gives the distance: 1″ of parallax is 1 parsec, 3.26 light-years.
Brown dwarf
An object too massive to be a planet but too small to shine like a star; cold ones glow mostly in infrared.
MJy/sr, µJy
Brightness units: MJy/sr for a patch of the image, µJy for the total light of one object.
σ (sigma)
A measure of how far a result is above the noise. 5σ means a chance fluctuation is extremely unlikely.
Flags
SPHEREx's own warnings for each pixel, such as saturated, cosmic-ray hit or ghost reflection.
Shift-and-add
Sliding images along a trial motion before stacking, so a faint moving object adds up into a sharp dot.
